# Raids on Mljet and Korčula, April 1944

## Overview

In late April 1944, the Yugoslav Partisans launched coordinated amphibious raids against German-held Dalmatian islands, striking at Mljet on April 21-22 and Korčula on April 22-24. These operations represented some of the most successful Partisan maritime operations of the war, demonstrating the growing capability of the Partisan Navy and amphibious forces to challenge German control of the Adriatic.

## Strategic Context

Following the German occupation of the Dalmatian islands in late 1943, the Wehrmacht maintained scattered garrisons across the island chain as part of their broader strategy to secure the Adriatic coastline and prevent Allied landings. However, these isolated garrisons proved vulnerable to amphibious assault. The Partisans, now operating from their stronghold on the island of Vis, coordinated with Allied forces to plan a series of raids designed to destroy German positions and disrupt their control of the coastal regions.

## The Raid on Mljet

The raid on Mljet was conceived as a diversionary operation to support a larger simultaneous assault on nearby Korčula. Roughly 900 Yugoslav Partisans from the 1st and 11th Dalmatian Brigades departed Vis on April 19, 1944, sailing in two convoys of motor-powered sailboats and small galleys, escorted by two lightly-armed naval vessels. The fleet faced significant logistical challenges—traveling only 4-5 knots, the Partisans could not reach Mljet in a single night. Instead, they staged through the island of Lastovo, departing on April 20 and landing on Mljet shortly after midnight on April 21.

The Partisans encountered a German garrison of approximately 200 men from the 750th Regiment of the 118th Jäger Division, positioned on fortified hills and in several settlements across the island. The assault began at dawn on April 21, and the Partisans quickly overran the German-held village of Radulići and the port of Sobra. However, fierce German resistance from well-fortified positions on Hills 514 and 488, surrounded by barbed wire and minefields, slowed the Partisan advance.

After hours of combat, during which the Partisans mounted multiple assaults, the Germans were eventually forced to abandon Hill 514 by early evening. However, a German counterattack recaptured the hill, forcing the Partisans to consolidate their positions. German reinforcements arrived during the night of April 21-22, but made no major assaults. Facing operational constraints and having achieved their primary objective of drawing German reserves away from Korčula, the Partisans began their retreat. On the night of April 22-23, they boarded their ships for evacuation, though their departure was complicated by collisions in the narrow cove, the loss of one armed vessel (the Enare II, which had to be scuttled), and severe weather conditions.

Despite these difficulties, the Partisans successfully evacuated their forces, casualties, and German prisoners of war. Partisan losses amounted to 25 killed and 76 wounded, while the Germans suffered 96 killed and 46 taken prisoner. More importantly for the larger strategic plan, the raid succeeded in diverting three companies of German reinforcements that might otherwise have reinforced Korčula.

## The Raid on Korčula

The primary objective of the April operations was the capture of Korčula, a larger and more strategically important island. On the night of April 22-23, while Partisan units were still evacuating from Mljet, the main force comprised approximately 1,800 troops from the 26th Dalmatian Division landed on Korčula in two assault groups. The southern group, commanded by Bogdan Stupar and consisting of elements of the 1st Dalmatian Brigade and supporting units, landed on the island's southern coast. A northern assault column under Borko Arsenić, commander of the 11th Dalmatian Brigade, struck from the north.

The German garrison, numbering approximately 1,600 men from the 118th Jäger Division with supporting artillery, was positioned around the fortified strongpoint at Blato, which dominated the island. The Partisan assault was initially concealed by fog, which allowed the attackers to approach their assault positions undetected. The Partisans achieved their initial objectives, quickly overrunning outer German defensive positions and capturing enemy weapons and ammunition.

However, the main German strongpoint at Blato proved stubbornly resistant. The Germans held well-constructed bunker systems, protected by barbed wire and minefields, and maintained organized machine gun and artillery fire. The Partisans mounted repeated assaults throughout the night of April 22-23, but all were repelled by the determined German defense. The decisive breakthrough came on April 23, beginning at 11:00 AM, when a coordinated assault, supported by Partisan artillery and mortars, finally broke German resistance. By late afternoon, German forces capitulated after suffering heavy casualties.

The results of the Korčula operation far exceeded the Mljet raid. German casualties were catastrophic: 297 killed and 439 captured. The Partisans seized significant materiel, including four 75mm howitzers, four anti-tank guns, ten heavy machine guns, 26 MG-42 machine guns, five 81mm mortars, five smaller mortars, and vast quantities of small arms ammunition. Partisan losses were 35 killed and 176 wounded.

## Significance

The successful raids on Mljet and Korčula marked a watershed moment in the Adriatic campaign. They demonstrated that, far from being isolated behind German lines, the Partisans could conduct complex amphibious operations with precision and achieve decisive results. Marshal Tito lauded the participating units, and even General Henry Maitland Wilson, the Allied Supreme Commander in the Mediterranean, sent personal congratulations, praising the Partisans' fighting abilities and success.

These operations forced the Germans to undertake substantial defensive improvements to their island garrisons, including enhanced artillery coverage, additional landing craft, and multiple layers of fortifications. They also inspired subsequent Partisan raids, including attacks on Šolta, Hvar, and Brač, establishing a pattern of successful amphibious operations that would characterize the final months of the Adriatic war. Most importantly, these raids reinforced German strategic defensive posture in Yugoslavia and demonstrated that control of the islands was increasingly contested, a development that would have implications for the broader Balkan campaign in the war's final year.
