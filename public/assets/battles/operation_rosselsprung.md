# Operation Rösselsprung / Raid on Drvar (25 May – 6 June 1944)

Operation Rösselsprung (German: "Knight's Move"; Serbo-Croatian: *Desant na Drvar*) was a German airborne and ground offensive launched on 25 May 1944 with the aim of capturing or killing Marshal Josip Broz Tito and destroying the Supreme Headquarters of the Yugoslav Partisan movement, based at the time in the town of Drvar in western Bosnia.

## Background

By the spring of 1944, the German 2nd Panzer Army had secured the Adriatic coast and most strategic points in the interior of occupied Yugoslavia through a series of large-scale winter operations, but had failed to deal a decisive blow to the Partisan movement. Rather than attempting once again to destroy Partisan units in the field, the German command decided instead to strike directly at the Supreme Headquarters, in the hope that removing Tito and his staff would cause the collapse of Partisan resistance. Intelligence placed Tito's headquarters in a cave above Drvar, and the Germans deliberately timed the attack for 25 May 1944 — Tito's official birthday — expecting to catch the Partisans off guard.

## Course of the Operation

The attack began at dawn on 25 May with a heavy air bombardment of Drvar, followed by a parachute and glider landing by the 500th SS Parachute Battalion in two waves. The landing went poorly from the start — one glider was shot down and three others crashed on landing, while further gliders and paratroopers were dropped well outside the intended drop zone. Fierce, chaotic fighting broke out through the town; German troops searching for Tito shot captured civilians and Partisan personnel indiscriminately, and the leadership of the local Communist Youth (SKOJ) committee was wiped out defending a single house.

Tito and Edvard Kardelj escaped the headquarters cave around 11:00, shortly before the arrival of the 3rd Lika Brigade, which had raced roughly 10 km from Trubar and immediately attacked the German paratroopers. The brigade's counterattack forced the German commander, Rybka, to consolidate his scattered forces, and by around 16:00 the surviving paratroopers were driven back to the town cemetery, where they dug in and held out overnight, suffering heavy losses, until relieved the next day by advancing German ground columns. Of over 800 paratroopers committed, only about 250 remained fit for combat by the following morning; the shattered 500th SS Parachute Battalion was later merged into a new 600th SS Parachute Battalion.

Simultaneously, German ground columns advanced on Drvar from several directions. A battle group of the 373rd Division advancing from Srb met determined resistance from the 2nd Lika Brigade but eventually broke through, reaching the outskirts of Drvar by 26 May. Further south and east, elements of the 7th SS Division and Chetnik units advancing from Banja Luka, Mrkonjić Grad, and Jajce were engaged by the 1st and 13th Proletarian Brigades, capturing Šipovo, Ključ, and Ribnik by 26 May.

With Drvar no longer safe, the Supreme Headquarters withdrew, screened by Partisan units fighting a running rearguard action through Glamočko polje. On 3 June, Tito, members of the Supreme Headquarters, Soviet General Korneyev, and the acting head of the British military mission were flown out by a Soviet aircraft from Kupreško polje to Bari, Italy; the remainder of the Supreme Headquarters was evacuated soon after by a British destroyer to the island of Vis. Heavy fighting between the German 7th SS Division and the Partisan 1st Proletarian Division continued for several more days before German attacks tapered off around 5–6 June, marking the end of the operation.

## Outcome

Operation Rösselsprung failed to achieve its central objective: Tito and the Supreme Headquarters escaped, and the Partisan movement's command structure remained intact. According to Partisan sources, losses from 25 May to early June amounted to 247 killed, 470 wounded, and 85 missing, against reported German losses of 343 killed, 881 wounded, and 135 missing. German wartime reports grossly exaggerated Partisan losses, claiming thousands killed or captured.

Although a tactical failure, the operation had lasting strategic consequences: rather than freeing German forces for use elsewhere, it triggered a further intensification of the war in Yugoslavia, tying down German Army Group F and its allied Chetnik and Ustaša auxiliaries in continuous anti-Partisan operations over the following months. The raid also prompted the Supreme Headquarters' permanent relocation to Vis under Allied protection, and led to greatly increased Allied air support for Partisan ground forces in the following weeks.
